What Is a Research Proposal?

A research proposal is a formal plan for your research project. Think of it as a blueprint — it lays out what you want to research, why it matters, and how you plan to do it. Writing this out in detail now will help you stay on track as you move into your annotated bibliography, literature review, and final paper.

The audience for this assignment is your discourse community — the group of people who care about your topic. Your job is to convince them that your research question is worth asking and that your plan for answering it makes sense. It's okay if some of your plans change as you go; what matters here is that you think through the details as carefully as you can right now.

→

How to Write the Proposal

Your proposal has five parts. Work through them in order — each one builds on the last. Be as explicit and detailed as possible throughout. This should be a thoughtful, complete plan, not a rough sketch.

Part 1

Your Topic

Introduce your topic and give some background on it. What is it? What do people already know or say about it? Define any key terms that someone unfamiliar with the topic would need to know. This section sets the stage for everything that follows.
Part 2

The Academic Conversation

Research doesn't happen in a vacuum. Scholars and writers are always responding to each other, building on previous work, and pushing back on ideas they disagree with. In this section, identify the conversation already happening around your topic. Where do people agree? Where do they disagree? What questions are still open? Showing that you understand this conversation is how you demonstrate that your research has a place in it.
Part 3

Your Connection and Motivation

Why does this topic matter to you? What have you observed, experienced, or noticed that drew you to this question? Write this section from your own perspective. Readers want to understand what is driving your curiosity, so be specific. The more honest and grounded this section is, the stronger your proposal will be.
Part 4

Your Research Questions and Methods

What do you actually want to find out? Write out your primary research question and any secondary questions that branch off from it. A good research question is specific enough to be answerable but open enough that it requires real investigation — it shouldn't be something you can just Google.

You should also describe here how you plan to answer your question. What methods will you use? Will you do textual analysis, interviews, surveys, or something else? What primary materials will you look at, and why are those the right ones for your project? Be specific about your choices and explain why they make sense for your research question.
Part 5

Discussion and Implications

This is where you address the "So what?" question. Why does your research matter? What contribution will it make to the conversation around your topic? Because you haven't finished your research yet, it's fine to speculate here — think about what you expect to find and why that would be significant. This section will grow into one of the most important parts of your final paper, so the more you develop it now, the better.
Part 6

Working Bibliography

List at least three sources you have already found that are relevant to your topic. These don't have to be your final sources — they are a starting point. For each one, write one sentence explaining what it contributes to your research. Format your citations in MLA.

Format Requirements

  • 3–4 pages, double-spaced (longer is fine if you need the space)
  • MLA format throughout
  • Working bibliography at the end (does not count toward page total)
  • Submit as a Word document (.docx) to Canvas
