Course Description

This course examines how video games tell stories — and asks students to become storytellers themselves. Through a combination of critical reading, close game play, and hands-on design work using the open-source platform Twine, students will analyze how narrative structures, player agency, and game mechanics work together to create meaning. By the end of the semester, students will have produced a portfolio of work that demonstrates their ability to think critically about narrative games and to design one of their own.

No prior coding experience is required. All games and software used in this course are free or available through library or streaming platforms. Watching a complete playthrough of a game on YouTube is equivalent to playing it for the purposes of this class.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

  1. Analyze how narrative structures and storytelling techniques function in video games using established theoretical frameworks
  2. Identify and apply core concepts — player agency, game mechanics, branching narrative, emotional pacing — in both critical writing and game design
  3. Design and develop interactive narratives using Twine, with attention to choice architecture, player experience, and narrative consequence
  4. Reflect critically on their own creative process as both player and designer
  5. Engage the field by reading and responding to foundational and contemporary scholarship in game studies and interactive narrative

Weekly Response Posts

15%  —  10 weeks × 1.5%

Ten times over the semester (not every week), students post a 150–200 word response to Canvas connecting a reading to a specific moment in one of the games we have played or watched. These are informal, low-stakes, and graded on completion and engagement, not argument quality. Think of them as a warm-up for discussion.

Close Play Essay

15%  —  700–900 words

An analytical essay in which you examine one specific mechanic in one specific game from our course list, and argue for how that mechanic reinforces, contradicts, or complicates the game's narrative. You must use at least one course reading as a theoretical frame. This essay is not a plot summary or a review — it is a focused argument about design and meaning. More detailed guidelines will be distributed in Week 4.

Paper Prototype

5%  —  3 pages

A low-stakes, three-page document in which you draft the skeleton of an original narrative game. Graded on completion and care, not polish. Full prompt distributed in Module 1, Week 4.

Twine Draft 1.0

10%

A playable Twine built from your Paper Prototype, with at least three meaningful player choices and two distinct endings. This does not need to be long or code-heavy — the focus is on narrative structure and player experience, not technical complexity.

Peer Review of Twine 1.0

5%

A structured written review of a classmate's Twine using a provided rubric. Reviewers address: how choice is presented, whether player agency feels meaningful, what the narrative does well and where it loses momentum.

Twine 1.0 Reflection

5%  —  1–2 pages

A reflection written after peer review. What did you learn from having someone play your game? What did you change or plan to change, and why? This is a metacognitive exercise — you are thinking about your design process, not just your game.

Group Game Presentation

10%  —  10–12 minutes + Q&A

In groups of 3–4, you will analyze Papers, Please (or an approved alternative) and present your findings to the class. Your presentation should address: how mechanics and narrative create tension together, what emotional response the game designs for, and how choice and consequence shape replayability.

Story Storyboard

5%

A planning document for your Final Twine — a visual or written map of your narrative structure, including branching logic, key decision points, and intended emotional arc. This is a scaffolding step, not a polished deliverable.

Final Twine Project

20%

A significantly expanded and revised Twine that builds on your earlier draft. The final game must demonstrate: meaningful narrative consequence for player choices, a coherent aesthetic and tonal identity, and at least two distinct endings that feel earned. Students may incorporate CSS, images, or conditional logic, but are not required to. The game will be shared in a brief in-class showcase.

Final Process Reflection

10%  —  3–4 pages

A reflection paper accompanying the Final Twine. Not a description of your game — a reflection on your process. How did your design thinking change across the semester? What readings, games, or feedback most shaped your decisions? What would you do differently?

Course Schedule

Module 1

Foundations of Narrative and Games

What does it mean for a game to tell a story — and why does it matter?
Week 1

Games as Storytelling Media

Reading
  • Battey, "Videogames Can, Do & Should Tell Stories"
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games — Introduction
Game

Watch or play: The Last of Us (at least the first two hours or equivalent traversal)

Discussion

How do video games tell stories differently than film or fiction? What does Battey mean when he says games are "inherently powerful" storytelling tools — and do you agree after watching The Last of Us?

Due: Weekly Response Post #1 — Agree or disagree with Battey using a specific moment from The Last of Us as your evidence.
Week 2

The Debate: Do Games Tell Stories at All?

Reading
  • Juul, "Games Telling Stories?" (open access)
  • Jenkins, "Game Design as Narrative Architecture" (open access)
Discussion

Juul and Jenkins represent two sides of an ongoing argument in game studies. Where do you land? Can a game tell a story without its mechanics getting in the way? Jenkins proposes thinking of designers as "narrative architects" rather than storytellers — what does that shift in framing open up?

Due: Weekly Response Post #2 — In 150–200 words, identify which framework (Juul or Jenkins) better describes one game you have played outside of class. Be specific.
Note: The Close Play Essay is introduced this week. Guidelines distributed. Topic proposals due Week 5.
Week 3

Space, Aesthetics, and Environment as Narrative

Reading
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games — Chapters 1 and 2
  • Zimmerman, "Narrative, Interactivity, Play, and Games"
Game

Watch or play: Firewatch (at least first hour)

Activity

In pairs, select a single scene or location from Firewatch and draw or describe a "narrative map" of that space — what story is being told by the environment itself, before any dialogue happens?

Discussion

How does aesthetics carry narrative information? What does the player feel before they are told anything?

Due: Weekly Response Post #3
Week 4

Player Agency and the Architecture of Choice

Reading
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games — Chapter 3
  • Murray, Hamlet on the Holodeck — Chapter 5: "Agency" (excerpt)
Discussion

Murray defines agency as "the satisfying power to take meaningful action and see the results of our decisions." What makes a choice feel meaningful versus arbitrary? How does Firewatch handle this?

Assigned: Paper Prototype
Paper Prototype Prompt

On page 1, write a scene (250–300 words) set in a specific, vivid location with at least one character and one source of tension. The player should feel grounded in a place.

On page 2, write two choices the player can make at the end of that scene. Think carefully: what does the player know, and what don't they know, when they make this decision?

On page 3, write what happens next for each choice — two separate continuations of equal care and weight. Due Week 6.

Module 2

Tools for Interactive Storytelling

Learning to make with Twine — and thinking through making.
Week 5

Introduction to Twine

Reading
  • Hahn, "Creating Digital Gamebooks with Twine"
  • Salter & Moulthrop, Twining — Chapter 1
Activity

In-class Twine workshop. We build the Paper Prototype's branching structure together in Twine. The goal for today is: get something playable. Don't worry about polish.

Discussion

What surprised you about moving from a Word document to Twine? What did the tool force you to think about differently?

Due: Weekly Response Post #4 — Reflect on your prior coding experience (or lack of it). What feels like a barrier? What doesn't?
Note: Close Play Essay topic proposals due this week (one paragraph: your game, your chosen mechanic, and the reading you plan to use).
Week 6

Branching Narrative and Player Interpretation

Reading
  • Finley, Branching Story, Unlocked Dialogue — Chapter 3
Game

Play or watch: Depression Quest (Zoe Quinn, 2013) — free in browser

Discussion

Depression Quest is built in Twine, and you can see its structure. How does Quinn use unavailable choices — options that are greyed out — as a narrative device? How does the game's word choice shape your interpretation of each decision?

Due: Paper Prototype (physical document)
Beginning: Twine Draft 1.0 — bring your Twine to class for workshop time
Week 7

Peer Review and Revision

Reading
  • Finley, Branching Story, Unlocked Dialogue — Chapter 6
  • Salter & Moulthrop, Twining — Chapter 2
Activity

Structured peer review workshop. In groups of three, each student plays their two partners' Twines and completes a written peer review form. Reviewers address: How are choices presented — does wording feel neutral, guiding, or manipulative? Does player agency feel meaningful? Where does the narrative lose momentum or clarity?

Due: Peer Review forms (written, submitted to Canvas)
Week 8

Reflection and Revision

Reading
  • Salter & Moulthrop, Twining — Chapter 3 (skim for practical reference)
Due: Twine Draft 1.0 (final version, submitted to Canvas)
Due: Twine 1.0 Reflection — 1–2 pages: What did peer review reveal? What are you changing, and why? What design decisions are you most uncertain about?
Module 3

Player Engagement, Emotion, and Consequence

What makes a game feel like it matters?
Week 9

Immersion, Agency, and Emotional Design

Reading
  • Murray, Hamlet on the Holodeck — Chapter 5 (continued/revisited)
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games — Chapter 4
Game

Begin watching or playing: Papers, Please

Discussion

Murray argues that a meaningful sense of agency is central to the pleasure of interactive narrative. How does Papers, Please create or complicate that agency? Can a game make you feel bad as a design goal?

Due: Weekly Response Post #5
Week 10

Moral Choice, Tension, and Replayability

Reading
  • Mitchell et al., "Storygameness: Understanding Repeat Experience and the Desire for Closure in Storygames"
Group Presentations

Papers, Please (or approved alternate game). Each group presents their analysis of how the game uses mechanics and narrative together to create tension, moral weight, and replayability. 10–12 minutes with Q&A.

Due: Weekly Response Post #6
Week 11

Emotional Pacing and Narrative Time

Reading
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games — Chapter 5
Game

Watch or play: To the Moon

Discussion

How does To the Moon use nonlinear time — moving backward through a character's life — as both a mechanic and an emotional strategy? What is the relationship between when the player learns something and how it feels to learn it?

Due: Close Play Essay
Week 12

Identity, Authorship, and Who Gets to Make Games

Reading
  • Anthropy, Rise of the Videogame Zinesters — Chapters 1–2 (excerpt)
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games — Chapter 8
Game

Watch or play: The Stanley Parable

Discussion

Anthropy argues that game-making tools like Twine have the potential to democratize who gets to tell stories in games. How does The Stanley Parable play with the relationship between player, designer, and narrator? What does it mean for you — as the person making a Twine this semester — to be an author?

Due: Weekly Response Post #7
Due (end of week): Story Storyboard — a visual or written map of your Final Twine's narrative architecture
Module 4

Final Project

Building, revising, and presenting your game.
Week 13

Workshop: Narrative Architecture

In-class work session. Bring your Twine-in-progress. The instructor will circulate for individual feedback. Agenda: Does your branching logic serve your narrative? Are your choices legible and meaningful? What does your ending(s) feel like to play?

Due: Weekly Response Post #8
Week 14

Workshop: Aesthetics and Tone

In-class work session focused on voice, aesthetic consistency, and player experience. Small group check-ins. Agenda: Does your tone stay consistent across passages? What does your Twine "look like" (even if it's plain text) — and is that a choice?

Due: Weekly Response Post #9
Week 15

Individual Conferences / Open Studio

Scheduled individual or small-group conferences with instructor. No whole-class meeting. Students continue developing final projects.

Week 16

Final Showcase and Reflection

In Class

Brief showcase. Each student (or paired group, by arrangement) shares their Final Twine for 5 minutes of open play, followed by a 2-minute description of one design decision they are proud of and one they would revisit.

Due: Final Twine Project
Due: Final Process Reflection (3–4 pages)
Due (before showcase): Weekly Response Post #10 — What is one thing a game we studied this semester taught you about your own design work?

A Note on the Weekly Response Posts

These posts are not essays. They are thinking-in-progress. The goal is to keep the reading and the playing in conversation with each other throughout the semester — rather than treating them as separate activities. You will not always have a fully formed argument. That is fine. What matters is specificity: cite a passage, name a moment in a game, make a connection. Graded on completion and genuine engagement (10 weeks × 1.5% = 15%).

Course Policies

Open-Source Philosophy

All required software (Twine) and most readings are free. Purchasing games listed on this syllabus is optional. Watching a complete playthrough on YouTube counts as playing for this course.

AI Writing Policy

This course is about your voice as a designer and thinker. AI-generated writing submitted as your own will receive a zero and be reported for academic integrity review. Using AI to brainstorm, prototype, or troubleshoot Twine code is acceptable with disclosure; using it to write your essays or reflections is not.

Accessibility

Students requiring accommodations should contact the instructor within the first two weeks. We will work together to ensure full participation.

Late Work

Extensions are available if requested before the deadline. Work submitted late without prior arrangement will be penalized 10% per day for up to five days, after which it will not be accepted.
