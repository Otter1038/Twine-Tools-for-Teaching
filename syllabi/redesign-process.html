Original Course Plan — Shared by Instructor

Teaching Narrative and Storytelling through Game Design

Learning Objectives

  1. Analyze the Role of Narrative in Games — critically examine how narrative structures and storytelling techniques are used in games to engage players and convey meaning.
  2. Apply Principles of Interactive Storytelling — design and develop interactive narratives using Twine, emphasizing branching storylines, player agency, and decision-making.
  3. Explore the Integration of Game Mechanics and Storytelling — investigate how mechanics influence storytelling, analyzing examples where mechanics reinforce or disrupt narrative cohesion.
  4. Design a Playable Prototype with Integrated Narratives — create a game prototype demonstrating the integration of narrative and mechanics.

Required Texts

  • Battey, "Videogames Can, Do & Should Tell Stories." Gamasutra, 2017.
  • Finley, T.K. Branching Story, Unlocked Dialogue. CRC Press, 2022.
  • Hahn, "Creating Digital Gamebooks with Twine." Mixed Reality and Games, 2020.
  • Mitchell et al., "Storygameness."
  • Reed et al., Adventure Games: Playing the Outsider. Bloomsbury, 2020.
  • Salter & Moulthrop, Twining. Amherst College Press, 2021.
  • Zimmerman, "Narrative, Interactivity, Play, and Games." Electronic Book Review, 2004.

Games

Depression Quest, Firewatch, Stanley Parable, Death and Taxes, Papers Please, To the Moon, Kentucky Route Zero, The Last of Us, This War of Mine

Course Structure (4 Modules)

Module 1: Introduction to Narrative Theory and Game Mechanics — Weeks 1–4. Covers storytelling in games, aesthetics, player agency, and narrative mechanics.
Module 2: Tools for Interactive Storytelling — Weeks 1–4. Introduces Twine, branching narrative, peer review, final Twine draft.
Module 3: Player Engagement — Weeks 1–4. Covers replayability, moral decisions, emotional engagement, choice and consequence.
Module 4: Final Project.

Frameworks Other Courses Use

Narratology vs. Ludology Debate

This is arguably the foundational theoretical conflict in the field, and it appears in nearly every comparable syllabus. The MIT OpenCourseWare "Introduction to Videogame Studies" course assigns Frasca's "Simulation versus Narrative: Introduction to Ludology" and Aarseth's "Doors and Perception: Fiction vs. Simulation in Games." Your course touches on it implicitly through Zimmerman, but engaging it directly could sharpen the theoretical backbone considerably.

Spatial Narratology / "Narrative Architects"

Henry Jenkins' "Game Design as Narrative Architecture" is one of the most widely assigned readings in the field. It would complement your Reed et al. readings on environment and aesthetics nicely.

Ludology Fundamentals

Vanderbilt's "Online Games: Literature, New Media, and Narrative" uses Jesper Juul's Half-Real as a key framework. Juul is almost universally assigned and would deepen your Module 1.

Structural Approaches Others Use

TTRPG as Scaffolding

John Laudun's ENGL 370 at Louisiana uses tabletop RPG design as a prerequisite step before digital game work — TTRPGs require clear writing and are a good place to experiment with mechanics without technical barriers.

Prototype + Analytical Essay Paired Together

Laudun's course pairs every creative prototype with an analytical essay: students first analyze a narrative game in writing, then build their own prototype. Analysis and making are treated as a single unit of work.

Replayability Tied to Theory Earlier

UT Dallas's ANGM 4369 foregrounds mechanics-as-narrative very early, using games like Citizen Sleeper and Slay the Princess to show how indie games disrupt conventional story expectations.

Notable Gaps Compared to the Field

  1. No Murray — Hamlet on the Holodeck is the most glaring absence. Her concepts of immersion, agency, and transformation are foundational vocabulary the whole field shares.
  2. No ludology/narratology debate — Even a short Juul essay ("Games Telling Stories?" is open access) would let students see the theoretical stakes.
  3. No historical IF — Courses at UCSC and Louisiana begin with text adventures (Zork, Colossal Cave) to show the lineage Twine work descends from.
  4. Limited cultural/identity critique — Courses in game studies are increasingly assigning Adrienne Shaw (Gaming at the Edge) or Anna Anthropy (Rise of the Videogame Zinesters) — the latter is especially relevant to Twine's history.
The course is well-constructed and has a distinctive voice. The biggest theoretical move that would bring it into fuller conversation with the field is threading in Murray and Jenkins alongside what you already have.

On Reading Weight for Upper-Division Undergrads

The short answer: some readings are fine, a few are mismatched, and the bigger issue is volume rather than difficulty.

What works well at this level

  • Battey and Zimmerman are genuinely accessible — Battey reads almost like a blog post, which is a smart entry point.
  • Reed et al. chapters, used selectively, are readable for upper-division students because they're grounded in specific games rather than dense theory.
  • Hahn's Twine piece is practical and low-barrier, which is exactly right for a tools module.

What may be too heavy or misaligned

  • Branching Story, Unlocked Dialogue (Finley) is written for industry practitioners, not undergraduates analyzing narrative. Six chapters may produce diminishing returns. Chapters 3 and 6 are most useful.
  • Salter and Moulthrop's Twining has a higher theoretical ceiling than it first appears. Using it strictly as a how-to resource is the right call.
  • Mitchell et al.'s "Storygameness" is the most academically dense piece. Assigning it without a reading scaffold could cause disengagement.

The real issue — overall volume

You're assigning readings AND asking students to play or watch full game traversals AND produce writing in almost every week. Consider alternating: text-heavy weeks with no new game, and game weeks with only a short critical framing piece.

Assignment Prompts from Comparable Courses

Close Play / Game Analysis Essays

Middlebury's FMMC 0282 "Videogames: Art/Culture/Medium" requires a 1,600–1,900 word analytical essay — a focused, scoped single-source analysis that teaches close reading before synthesis. The Utah "Narrative in Game Design" course: ~40% of the grade comes from playing and writing about games, 20% from writing about readings, 40% from a final Gamebook.

Design Documents as Capstone

UBC's CRWR 310 uses a Narrative Design Document (worth 30%) requiring students to produce a full design document covering story structure, characters, world, and mechanics in one integrated artifact.

Gamebook / IF as Final with Visible Scaffold

The Utah course's final is a complete Gamebook scaffolded through: Preliminary Proposal, Flowchart, Plot Outline, Character Outline, and Setting Outline — each due separately before the final submission.

Gender/Identity Twine Assignment

One widely circulated assignment from Digital Pedagogy in the Humanities asks students to create a Twine engaging gender politics and identity — students propose a game addressing a gender studies issue, conduct research, then develop the game.

Practical Takeaways

  1. Trim Finley to excerpts. Two chapters (rather than six) — let the platform do the rest of the teaching.
  2. Name and grade the scaffolding steps. Give formal names and point values to intermediate deliverables — Flowchart, Character Outline, Story Map — before the final Twine.
  3. Add one short analytical essay tied to a single game. Something like: "In 600–800 words, analyze how one mechanic in Depression Quest reinforces or contradicts its narrative themes" — scoped, arguable, and directly teachable using your existing readings.

Key Design Decisions

On reading weight

The redesigned reading load alternates intentionally. Text-heavy weeks (2, 4, 9, 12) have shorter or no game assignments. Game weeks (1, 3, 6, 11) have lighter reading — usually one open-access piece rather than a book chapter.

What was added and why

Jenkins and Juul go in Week 2 as a paired debate — the ludology/narratology argument gives students theoretical vocabulary that pays dividends across the whole semester. Murray's "Agency" chapter appears twice (Weeks 4 and 9), intentionally: students encounter it before they've built anything, then revisit it midway through when they're actively designing. The Anthropy excerpt lands in Week 12 to directly address the cultural gap noted in the original conclusion, and it pairs well with The Stanley Parable's self-aware authorship themes.

What was cut or trimmed

Finley goes from six chapters to two (3 and 6) — the most practically useful for undergrad designers. Half-Real was replaced with Juul's shorter open-access essay "Games Telling Stories?" to keep the reading load appropriate.

On the assignments

The Paper Prototype, Twine 1.0, Peer Review, Reflection, Storyboard, and Final Twine are now named, graded, and sequenced as a visible scaffold (10% + 5% + 5% + 5% + 20% + 10%). The Close Play Essay is a genuinely new assignment type — analysis-only, no making — which gives students a different kind of thinking and provides a formative window into how they're reading the games before they have to design.

On grading weight

Weekly Response Posts stay at 15% but are made explicit (×10 posts, low-stakes, graded on engagement not argument quality). The Final Twine carries 20% — enough to feel significant without making the whole course hinge on one deliverable.
