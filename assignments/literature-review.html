What Is a Literature Review?

In your research proposal, you mapped out a plan. In your annotated bibliography, you gathered and evaluated your sources individually. Now it's time to bring those sources together into one unified document. A literature review synthesizes the existing research on your topic — that means you're not just summarizing what each source says, but showing how they talk to each other: where they agree, where they disagree, and what questions are still open.

Think of it as drawing the map of the conversation your research is entering. By the end of this assignment, a reader should understand the landscape of your topic and see exactly where your own research fits into it.

→

How to Write the Literature Review

Your literature review has five components. Each one builds toward a complete, synthesized picture of your topic's research landscape.

Step 1

Research

Find 6–8 scholarly sources — peer-reviewed journal articles, books, or materials from academic databases — that address different aspects of your topic. Your sources should represent a range of viewpoints or approaches, not just sources that all say the same thing. The goal is to show the breadth of the conversation, including where researchers see things differently.
Step 2

Synthesis

This is the core of the assignment. Synthesis means moving beyond summary — you're showing how the sources connect to each other and to your research question. For each source, ask yourself:
  • What are the key findings or arguments?
  • How does this source contribute to the broader understanding of the topic?
  • Where does it agree or disagree with other sources you've found?
  • Does it fill a gap, challenge an assumption, or raise a new question?
Step 3

Organization

A literature review is not a list of summaries — it's a structured argument about what the research shows. Organize your review in whichever way makes the most sense for your topic. Common approaches include grouping sources by theme, by methodology, or by chronological order. Whatever structure you choose, make sure the reader can follow your logic from one section to the next.
Step 4

Critical Reflection

Toward the end of your review, step back and reflect on the research as a whole. Where does the scholarship agree? Where does it conflict? What has been left unexplored or understudied? This section shows that you're not just reporting what others have said — you're thinking critically about it. This is also where you can begin to signal how your own research fits into or responds to what you've found.
Step 5

Formatting and Citation

Follow MLA citation style throughout. Your review should be clearly written, well-organized, and free of grammatical errors. All sources cited in the body of your review must appear in your Works Cited page at the end.

Format Requirements

  • 4–5 pages, double-spaced, MLA format
  • 6–8 scholarly sources (peer-reviewed articles, books, or academic databases)
  • Sources must represent a range of viewpoints or approaches
  • Works Cited page at the end (does not count toward page total)
  • Submit as a Word document (.docx) to Canvas
