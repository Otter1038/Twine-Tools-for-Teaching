What Is the Final Research Paper?

A researched journal article is a genre that allows scholars to present their findings from primary research and make claims that answer very specific research questions. Your final paper works the same way — it is the culmination of everything you have done this semester.

Now that you have collected all of your primary and secondary sources, it's time to put your research together and consider what you've learned. The goal is to confidently pose answers to your research questions using all of the data you have collected. You will open with the introduction you developed in your Research Proposal, revised and strengthened using everything you've learned since then.

Required Sections

Your paper must include the following five sections in order. Each one builds on the work you've already done this semester.

Section 1

Introduction. Begin with the introduction you drafted in your Research Proposal, revised and updated to reflect everything you have learned since then. Your introduction should draw readers into the conversation around your topic by synthesizing your secondary sources, clarify the specific niche your project occupies within that conversation, and end with a clear statement of your research question(s) and what your paper will argue.

Use the feedback you received on your Literature Review to strengthen this section before submitting your final draft.

Section 2

Methods. Explain how you collected your primary data. Describe what you did, who or what was involved, and why you made the choices you made. Your methods section should be detailed enough that someone else could replicate your study.

What methods did you use — interviews, textual analysis, observation, surveys? Who were your participants, and why did you select them? What texts or materials did you collect, and why were they the right ones for your research question?

Section 3

Results. Analyze your primary data and report your key findings. For each piece of data, explain what you found and how it helped you better understand your topic. Use quotes from interviews, examples from texts, and descriptions from observations to support your analysis.

Open this section with your coding table(s) so readers understand how you analyzed your data. Note: in this section you are reporting what you found, not yet connecting it to secondary sources — that comes in the Discussion.

Section 4

Discussion. This is where everything comes together. Synthesize your primary data and your secondary sources to make and support your central claim — your answer to your research question(s). All of your primary data should be represented here, with at least two secondary sources used to connect your findings back to the broader scholarly conversation.

Open this section with a clear paragraph that reminds readers of your research question(s), the scholarly conversation you entered, and the claim you are making. Use subsections to organize your argument if they make the structure clearer.

Section 5

Conclusion. End your paper with any conclusions that didn't fit earlier, followed by suggestions for future research. How could your research be further developed? What other materials or conversations might lead to a more complete understanding of your topic? What are the limitations of your study that someone else could address?

Format Requirements

Submission Standards

  • 10+ pages, double-spaced, Times New Roman 12pt, 1" margins
  • MLA or APA format throughout — be consistent with whichever you choose
  • Section headings required (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion)
  • Works Cited or References page at the end (does not count toward page total)
  • Appendices on separate pages after Works Cited — must include consent forms and interview transcripts
  • Submit as a Word document (.docx) to Canvas
A Note on Appendices. Each appendix goes on its own page after your Works Cited. You are required to include a consent form and a full transcript for every interview you conducted. Any other appendices — observation notes, coded transcripts, images of analyzed texts, large tables — are at your discretion, but readers benefit from seeing your coded data.
