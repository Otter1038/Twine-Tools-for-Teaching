What Is an ePortfolio?

Your ePortfolio is a personal website where you showcase what you learned this semester. It's not just a place to upload files — it's a space where you explain, demonstrate, and reflect on your growth as a writer. Think of it as the argument you make for your own learning.

Because learning happens over time, your ePortfolio should grow with you throughout the semester. The best ePortfolios are built week by week, not assembled at the end. You'll have five check-ins along the way to keep you on track.

→

What Goes In It

Your ePortfolio has three main components: your student learning outcomes, your major writing assignments, and your writing artifacts. Each piece needs reflection — not just the work itself, but your thinking about it.

Component 1

Student Learning Outcomes

ENC 1102 is built around six student learning outcomes. Your ePortfolio should address each one. For every outcome, explain what it means in plain terms, show how your writing demonstrates that you engaged with it, and reflect on what you learned. Use examples from your actual work — don't just describe the outcome in the abstract.

The six outcomes are: Generating Inquiry, Multiple Ways of Writing, Information Literacy, Research Genre Production, Contributing Knowledge, and Revision.

Component 2

Major Writing Assignments

Include all four major assignments in your ePortfolio. For each one, embed or link the full document and write a reflection that contextualizes the work — what was the assignment asking you to do, how did you approach it, what did you learn from it?

For at least one major assignment, include multiple drafts (for example, Draft 1, Draft 2, and Final). Walk readers through the feedback you received and the specific revisions you made. This is one of the most important parts of your ePortfolio — it shows that writing is a process.

Component 3

Writing Artifacts

Include at least five writing artifacts representing at least three different kinds of writing tasks. These can include discussion posts, reading responses, peer review feedback, free writes, or any other writing you did this semester. For each one, explain why you chose it and what it shows about your learning.

Screenshots are encouraged — especially when you want to highlight a specific moment, comment, or revision that would be hard to capture otherwise.
